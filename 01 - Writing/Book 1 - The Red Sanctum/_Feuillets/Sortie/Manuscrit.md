# Chapter 1

Look : I didn’t wake up this morning thinking *“You know what sounds fun ? Blowing a chunk out of the Museum of Natural History !”*, but here we are. Chaos and I are kind of on a first-name basis.

Magic isn’t supposed to be real. It’s the kind of thing you see in books and at the movies. Heroes, princesses, wizards, all of that. But life’s full of curve balls, and this is yours : Magic ? Totally real. And it’s always arm in arm with trouble.

Have you ever seen something in the corner of your eye that just *wasn’t right*? Like the world tilted for a second and you glimpsed the impossible ? Or maybe you got tangled up in something unexplainable and weird. Those times ? Real. One hundred percent. And they weren’t accidents either.

You have magic. That’s the only way you’re reading this book. But even if you aren’t aware of it, that doesn’t mean it’s too late. So here’s your last chance.\
If you want to live your life in blissful ignorance, here’s your cue to exit. Seriously. This is the red light. Hit the brakes, close this book, toss it in the trash, or burn it.    \
Whatever you do is up to you.

But…if you want answers. If you’re willing to face the consequences and tear down the curtain to see the wizard behind it, then keep reading.\
But buckle up, because this ride doesn’t come with seat belts.

My name is Alexandra Paige, but please just call me “Alex”. No one calls me Alexandra unless I’m in trouble—and that happens enough already. I’m fifteen, and my life is basically the greatest hits album of “Schools I’ve Been Expelled From” written and composed by yours truly. I’ve lost track of how many. Every year, sometimes more, something happens. And by “happens”, I mean it’s always blamed on me, and I’m shown the door.

“This year will be different,” my Dad had told me. I wanted to believe him, but my track record wasn’t great.

See, when I say stuff “happens”, I’m not talking about normal teenage drama. I’m not cheating on tests, skipping classes, or stuffing kids into lockers. Nope, my expulsions are always for what I’ve coined as *“mysterious happenings”*. It’s when mysterious things…happen. Duh.

Take sixth grade for example. These kids had cornered me in the cafeteria, yelling about how I *totally* sabotaged one of their science fair projects—apparently their volcano exploded a little too well. I kept saying that it wasn’t me and that the volcano happened to explode when I walked by. Surprise, surprise, no one believed me. Consequently, I got shoved into a vending machine. The machine then “malfunctioned” and a torrent of carbonated beverages came flooding out of it, pelting them as they ran away. I thought it was funny—until I was expelled for *hacking* the vending machine. *The Soda Witch*, they called me.

I could go on and on with examples, but I’ll just give you one more.

Fast forward to last year. School Football Championship. I was helping to set up fireworks for the halftime show when some *genius* thought it would be a great idea to dump ice-cold Gatorade down my back. Ice cubes down your spine ? *Not cool*. I screamed, blacked out, and when I woke up, I was in the middle of a circle of scorched grass. Guess the fireworks went off a little early, and half the football team ended up in the hospital. Naturally, I was the only one without a scratch, so they blamed me. I was expelled for “explosive behavior”.

You see the pattern right ? Stuff happens all around me—stuff I can’t explain—and somehow, I’m always labeled the bad guy. Wrong place, wrong time ; the story of my life.

Spoiler alert : It hasn’t gotten better with time.

I found myself at yet another “institution”. A boarding school in Rhode Island called Harmony Hills Academy.

“It’s to help you build character,” my Dad told me.

Because apparently, the solution to my life falling apart was exile. Again. It felt like he was keeping me at arm’s length, as if I came with a big sign on my forehead that yelled *danger*. The only real memories I had of us actually being close were during the summers at the cabin. Those were the golden days—hot chocolate by the fire, s’mores, lazy nights under the stars. It was like whatever cosmic prankster that was messing with my life decided to take summers off.

So back to Harmony Hills, which by the way, was anything *but* harmonious. It was less a “school” and more of a low-budget prison. My dorm was a shoe box I shared with three other girls who couldn’t have cared less if I’d vanished into the void. They never bothered to learn my name, though *honestly*, I never learned theirs either. The walls of the academy were a depressing shade of gray, with peeling plaster that made the whole place feel like a shell after a vampire sucked all the life out of you.

And don’t get me started on the halls. Narrow, claustrophobic, and littered with old propaganda posters—Uncle Sam glaring down at me with this *“I Want You”* finger pointed at me. I swore that his eyes would follow me, tempting me to enlist just to get out of there.

Oh, the staff ? They were a delightful mix of incompetent fools and military wannabes. You either had teachers who were so clueless they couldn’t keep control of a kindergarten class, or drill sergeants who acted like every class was *boot camp*. You had to salute and everything. They patrolled the halls like wardens, always on the lookout for someone to yell at. And you better pray you never saw “detention”, which was less “stay after school” and more “enjoy solitary confinement”. The rumors were that it was a padded room with just a desk and a pencil to write with. A House Hunter’s dream room.

The cafeteria food wasn’t much better. Imagine the color gray. Now imagine that every single meal was that color. Pizza ? Gray. Chicken ? Gray. Chocolate cake ? Still gray. I got really good at eating pasta with my eyes closed just to avoid thinking about what it looked like.

I haven’t even gotten to the worst part. Rather than the food, the wardens, or the soul-sucking atmosphere, the thing I hated most was the *pretending*. Everyone was doing it. Teachers pretending to care. Students pretending they were happy. The whole place pretending that we weren’t in a grayscale hellhole. It was infuriating.

There was, however, one shining mote of color in the gray, placid tones of Harmony Hills : Mr. Terrell. Somehow, this beacon of human decency ended up teaching history here. He was…different. He was kind. Oh, and he smiled (Seriously, a teacher who could smile ! Who would have thought ?). He looked young too—early twenties, maybe—with hazel eyes, shoulder-length brown hair, and a frame that looked like a stiff breeze might knock him over.

While history wasn’t my favorite subject, his class was the one place I felt that I could breathe. Like an enchantment had been placed around the door-frame and every time I walked in was a breath of fresh air. Like I hadn’t seen the sunlight in months. There was also something about the way he talked—a calm, soothing melody that seemingly only I could hear. The rest of the class ignored Mr. Terrell. They didn’t care. He was just another teacher to step on. Though they never did. Everyone was well-behaved in his class, whether they listened to him or not.

I listened. Or at least, I tried. The thing is, I’m not great at listening to people monologue. It all just turns into that Charlie Brown trumpet noise.

“Bwah, bwah bwah bwah bwah.” You get the idea.

Six months. That’s how long I endured this hellish place. Six months of gray walls, gray food, and gray days, with the only splash of color being Mr. Terrell’s boring history class.

Then we went on a field trip, and everything changed.

Harmony Hills field trips are lovingly called “retreats”—because it’s the only time we get to escape the lifeless walls of the academy. Watching the teachers try to wrangle us into buses was pure entertainment. Imagine three overstuffed sardine cans and you’ve got the picture.

I got a window seat, which would have normally been a win, but when has the universe ever loved me enough for that ? I looked to my side to see the worst possible person sitting next to me : Harvey Dunn.

Harvey was short and pudgy—not quite rotund, but close enough. “Big-boned” maybe. His undercut hairstyle really emphasized the roundness of his face, and the five o’clock shadow he always sported made him look like a high schooler who had failed way too many classes. I might have *believed* the rumors that he was actually older than us if I hadn’t seen him bawling his eyes out last week about wanting to go home and see his “mommy”. Here’s the rundown : he’s underhanded and slimy. He’s always looking to prank teachers or torment someone who wasn’t a part of his “squad”. He was a bully who was good at pretending to be a *golden boy*.

Harvey plopped into the seat with a thud, his smirk turning towards me. He could obviously see the disdain that was written on my face like a neon sign.

“What up, dweeb ?”

“Oh, I’m sorry,” I said, feigning shock and shielding my eyes with one hand. “I was just wondering if your face came with a warning label. Or were we all supposed to figure it out the hard way ?”

“What did you just say about my face ?” Harvey bellowed, the facade fading as he stood back into the aisle and jabbed a crooked finger in my direction. “Say that again and I’ll—”

“What’s going on over there, Dunn ?” boomed Mr. Morris, our fitness instructor, from across the aisle. See : *boot camp instructor.

Harvey froze mid-threat. His face twisted and contorted into the kind of smile that belongs on a cheap department store mannequin. In a sugary tone that would give anyone diabetes, he said :

“Oh nothing, sir. I was simply asking Alex if we might switch seats so that I could see out the window.”

“Enough of that,” Mr. Morris barked. “Sit down and stay quiet. That goes for you too, Paige.”

I saluted with all the sarcasm I could muster, sticking my tongue out at Harvey as he plopped back down.

“This is why you don’t have friends,” he muttered.

I didn’t bother replying. Sometimes, silence is louder than a comeback. Plus—I didn’t have one anyway. I turned to the window and let Harvey’s snide remarks fade into background noise. Out of all the students, Harvey Dunn got on my nerves the most. Not only was he the most fake person I’d ever met in my life, but he had a talent for digging up whatever hurt most and then twisting the knife to rub it in. And the worst part ? He wasn’t wrong. I didn’t have friends. Not here, not anywhere. I was never around long enough to have them.

Two and a half hours later, we still didn’t know where we were going. The teachers herded us onto the bus without so much as a hint, but as the skyline and concrete buildings came into view, I knew instantly ; New York City. I’d lived there a couple of years since that’s where my Dad worked. The buildings in the distance made me feel nostalgic, like flipping through a sepia-toned scrapbook of memories with my Dad in his tiny apartment on 93rd.

He still lived there, on the East Side. It was close to work.

*And far away from me*, I thought.

I shook the thought away, muttering a curse under my breath. There wasn’t enough time to chide myself for my thoughts as Mr. Terrell stood unsteadily at the front of the bus.

“As you can see,” he began, “we’re heading to New York.”

A few gasps from the Barbie Brigade in the back of the bus made me roll my eyes. I called them that because they reminded me of those glitter-covered dolls in toy aisles—plaster smiles, plastic everything. Definitely not fantastic like the boxes said.

“Now,” Mr. Terrell continued, “when we get to the museum, I want you all on your best behavior.”

A museum ? Of course, what else would it be ? Mr. Terrell was in charge of the trip, after all. I’d been to plenty during my time in the city, but there was one my Dad always avoided. He acted like it was cursed or something, so I always had this longing to go there.

“Um, where are we going ?” I asked, raising my hand awkwardly.

Mr. Terrell turned to me with that smile he always gave us right before a pop-quiz.

“The Museum of Natural History.”

Bingo.

The bus pulled to a stop thirty minutes later, and even from a distance, the building loomed over us. The entrance was framed by skyline-sized marble columns, each carved with intricate depictions of ancient creatures—dinosaurs frozen mid-roar, primordial birds caught mid-flight and gnarled trees stretching skyward as if to claw at the heavens. The square in front of the building was anchored by a massive stone pedestal, atop which stood a green, oxidized, copper statue. Its weathered surface tried its best to gleam in the sunlight, but time was a cruel mistress. From here, I could just make out the figure of someone in a cloak with a wide-brimmed hat holding a staff aloft in a triumphant pose.

They herded us off the bus one by one, double-checking a head count as if we could have lost someone *on the way*. At the other end of the line, Harvey was aiming a piece of gum at some poor girl’s hair. It would take her hours to get it out. I knew from experience.

Now that we were closer, I got a better look at the statue—and froze. It wasn’t the same anymore. When I first saw it, I could have sworn that it was a single person. Now, there were three : a man on horseback flanked by two Native Americans holding spears.

“Wasn’t that statue wearing a hat ?”

The words slipped out with an uncomfortable laugh before I could stop it. Mr. Terrell glanced up from his itinerary sheet, his expression molding into something that wasn’t quite concern. Confusion ?

“Excuse me ?” he asked.

“Sorry, I just…” I scrambled for the words to explain without sounding like I needed to be put in “detention”. “I thought I saw a hat on the statue earlier. Must have been a bird or something.”

Mr. Terrell’s gaze sharpened to a razor’s edge, his eyes narrowing as though trying to read my mind. It felt like he was silently willing me to spout my deepest secrets. Then he said something. Well, his mouth moved at least, but I didn’t hear anything. Only silence greeted me at the door, as though my ears had been left on the nightstand.

“Huh ?” was all I managed in response.

*Eloquent as ever, Alex.*

“Nothing,” he said, snapping his focus back to the group. “Time to head inside. Remember—behave !”

I gasped as we walked through the grand entrance and into the lobby. The interior was breathtaking ; it was like the museum was playing tricks on physics and was somehow bigger on the inside. Towering columns mirrored the Roman-inspired architecture from outside and their stone surfaces climbing nearly fifty feet before curving into a glass dome overhead. The dome was intricately detailed with a celestial map that shimmered as if the stars were actually moving. Maybe there were some hidden electronics up there or something, but it was still mesmerizing.

And then there were the dinosaurs.

Two colossal displays dominated the center of the room, perched atop large, artificial rocks. The first was a long-necked giant, stretching so high it nearly brushed up against the stars. It reared back on its hind legs in such a dramatic pose that I half expected it to come alive like that one movie I saw. Beside it, on the other rock, was a smaller skeleton—a raptor, I guessed—that was mid-pounce, jaws frozen in a vicious snarl. Its jagged teeth were poised to sink into the first thing—or person—it was lucky enough to get its hands on. I guess dinosaurs didn’t have hands. *Claws*, then ? Anyway, I knew raptors were pack hunters (thanks, Mr. Doyle’s fifth-grade class), so I looked around expecting a few more to be lurking nearby. No dice ; just the one.

Slightly disappointed, I shifted my gaze to the rest of the room.

Beyond the displays was a circular desk with a sign overhead : “Tours and Information”. Behind it stood a woman with a baby-blue suit, her impossibly cheerful smile stretched ear-to-ear. She was handing out the same boring pamphlets we’d already been given. The way her expression never faltered was creepy. She either REALLY loved her job, or her paycheck was big enough to fake it that convincingly.

Farther back, the lobby branched into a circular hub with hallways leading off in every direction. Above each entryway was a sign : “Biodiversity Hall”,  “Hall of Ocean Life”,  “Birds of the World”, “#%#@# %& #^$%@&%”. Huh ? What was that ? There was one last sign, but I couldn’t seem to read it. Every time I tried to focus on it, the letters blurred like the details of a dream slipping away the moment you wake up. My head started to ache—a low, familiar rumbling, the kind I got whenever something strange was happening. It wasn’t unbearable yet, but I had a feeling it was only going to get worse.

Mr. Terrel’s voice pulled me away from my thoughts as he waved us over to one side of the room.

“Students, this is our guide for the tour, Mrs. Jin.”

The guide in question was tall and lanky, with an obviously fake tan and long black hair that cascaded past her shoulders. She wore a sleek black dress that was way too fancy for the job while carrying a tablet in one hand and waving to us with the other. Her smile was as unsettling as the woman’s at the desk, only this time hidden under like six layers of makeup. She looked like she’d been plucked straight from a Glamour magazine and over-polished to perfection. I hated her already.

After her *enthusiastic* greeting, Mr. Jin explained the plan : a few hours touring the museum, a break for lunch, then more touring before the bus ride back. I wasn’t paying much attention, though. My head was still hurting, the dull ache turning into a rhythmic drill. It was as though the collective thoughts of everyone around me were tunneling into my brain. My stomach churned and I knew I had to get out of there.

I turned to Mr. Morris, who was standing nearby.

“Can I go to the bathroom ?”

Big mistake. Mr. Morris might have been a great Harvey wrangler, but that didn’t mean he was nice. He sighed loudly, clearly annoyed that I would have the *audacity* to ask something of him. He flagged down an attendant and made them show me the way. The boy’s expression said it loud and clear :

*This is NOT part of my job description.*

After a pointed glare from Mr. Morris, though, he complied. I felt bad for him, but the relief of escaping this crowd and cooling my head was worth it.

As soon as I was away from everyone the headache started to fade. In the bathroom, I splashed cold water on my face and let out a deep breath. The girl staring back at me in the mirror looked about as tired as I felt. My gray hair was a mess. “Like silver,” my dad would say. It was the only thing I had left of my mother, and it felt more like a curse. I’d tried dyeing it before—many times—but nothing ever stuck. It was like my hair was enchanted or something, stubbornly refusing every attempt to style or change it. Cutting it off to my shoulders was about the only thing I COULD do.

A knock at the door startled me away from my thoughts.

“Um, excuse me ?” the attendant called, his tone half-apologetic, half-impatient.

“Yeah, I’m coming.”

I took one last look at myself in the mirror, sighed, and wandered out of the bathroom back to this “retreat”.

When I got back to the lobby, I distinctly noticed that my group of rowdy teenagers were nowhere in sight. I glanced back to the attendant, but he was gone—vanished like Batman. Impressive ? Sure. Helpful ? Not even a little.

I took a few laps around the lobby, scanning for anyone remotely familiar. No luck. I could’ve just grabbed another attendant and had them help me, but no thanks. Talking to random strangers ? Hard pass. I’d rather pick a random direction and go.

So I did. I took a gamble and picked a random hallway, with no idea what I was about to walk into. Maybe if I’d waited, things would’ve turned out differently. Too late now.

I wasn’t paying too much attention to the exhibits as I briskly walked, trying to avoid getting caught and risking yet another suspension. Still, a few things flickered in my peripheral vision : skeletons, wax statues, mannequins, a wizard—wait what ? I stopped dead and backtracked, my eyes locking in on an exhibit.

I swore that I’d seen an old, robed man holding a staff and wearing a classic pointy wizard hat. But now ? Now it was a mannequin of a young guy in medieval armor, his sword raised high like he was ready to storm a castle. The plaque read *“The Great War of Aldham”*. I’d never heard of it before, but the name sent a needle-sharp jolt into the back of my head like I was supposed to recognize it.

Shaking off the weirdness, I kept walking, this time paying closer attention to the other exhibits around me. The farther I went, the stranger it all felt—like the air was thickening and the back of my head was itching.

I stopped at another display : a diorama of cowboys mid-shootout. The wild mix of knights and cowboys in the same museum didn’t make any sense, but the details were impressive. Then I saw something.

“Wait, what ?”

I stepped closer, nearly leaning over the rope barrier to get a closer look. The scene had two sides : a saloon front on the right and a stack of boxes on the left. Figures were frozen in action-like poses, weapons drawn. A few were even dramatically sprawled on the ground, covered in red. But it was a figure way in the back that caught my attention.

He wasn’t holding a gun. In fact, he wasn’t holding anything. His hand was outstretched, two fingers pointed to make a finger gun at the enemy. A tattoo peeked out from under the sleeve of the arm he had up—a dark black line that wrapped around his wrist and intricate symbols that made their way up his arm and into the sleeve. *Deja-vu* hit me hard. I’d seen the tattoo before ; it was familiar. But from where ? Before I could think any further, a sudden and sharp pain pierced the back of my skull, like the world’s worst brain freeze. I grabbed my head and yelped.

It felt like I was about to black out, but a voice cut through all the haze, snapping me back.

“And over here you can see…”

Mr. Terrell. The pain ebbed as I turned towards the sound and away from the exhibit. I followed the voice, rounding a corner to see a group of classmates gathered around him. I breathed a sigh of relief as I slipped back into the group.

Something was off, though. Everyone’s attention was glued to Mr. Terrell, who was now leading the tour. But where was *Mrs. Six Layers of Foundation*? He spoke animatedly, pointing at an exhibit—but the words he was saying didn’t reach me. Instead, my vision began to blur while looking at the exhibit and the dull ache at the back of my head came roaring to the front.

The thundering pulse of my heartbeat filled my ears, drowning out everything else. My body screamed that something was wrong—goosebumps prickled my skin, and the hairs on the back of my neck stood on end.

“Mr. Terrell,” I croaked, raising my hand. “What are you talking about ?”

I’d hoped that getting my attention away from the pain would help. What I didn’t expect ? The room to freeze. Mr. Terrell’s head snapped toward me, eyes wide like a deer in headlights. For the first time ever, Mr. Terrell looked…afraid ? I lowered my hand, regretting asking a question, but it was too late. He was already next to me, his gaze sharp and unyielding.

“What are you doing here ?” he demanded.

I blinked, stunned into silence. The conviction in his voice made my heart lurch, the thundering in my ears building to a deafening crescendo.

“I…I don’t…” I stammered, the words clumping in the back of my throat.

“You have to go back,” he said, his tone urgent, almost pleading. “I don’t know how you found us, but it’s dangerous for you to be here. Go back.”

“I don’t understand,” I said, my voice barely above a whisper. “What’s going on ?”

“Go !” he snapped. This was the first time I’d heard Mr. Terrell yell, and it wasn’t a pleasant experience. The look in his eyes still wasn’t anger—it was fear.

And that fear transferred directly to me. My instincts were screaming at me to run. So I did—or at least, I tried. I barely managed to turn around before colliding with another student, sending us both sprawling to the floor.

“Ow ! Watch it !” they yelped.

“S-Sorry,” I stammered, scrambling to my knees and reaching out to help. But when I looked at them—I froze.

They had purple skin. Yeah, *purple*. They had horns that twisted into sharp loops and points that screamed *danger*. Vertical slits where their eyes should have been. My lungs emptied in a single, panicked, gasp as I fell back to my butt in horror.

“What are you looking at ?” they snapped, like I was the one being weird.

I couldn’t answer. My chest heaved as my vision seemed to blur and glitch, flipping between a normal teenager and…whatever this was.

“Alex ? Alex, are you okay ?” Mr. Terrell crouched beside me, his hand outstretched. He looked normal—thank god—but I shoved him aside anyway, desperate to get away.

The chaos only escalated from there as I got to my feet. My right eye saw things as they should have been : normal, everyday students, staring at me like I was having a mental breakdown—which I was. But my left eye ?

Monsters.

Everywhere.

One girl had pointed ears. The guy next to her ? Scales like a lizard. And the one casually checking their phone and ignoring all of this ? A cat tail swishing behind them like it was no big deal.

It was a *very* big deal.

I spun on my heel, only to come face-to-face with a wall of fur. Slowly—*very* slowly—I looked up.

It was no wall. It was a bull.

Not the “moo” kind. This one stood on two legs, towering at least seven feet tall. Horns that curved forward like finely sharpened weapons and beady black eyes that bored into my soul.

So, obviously, I screamed.

And everything went white.
