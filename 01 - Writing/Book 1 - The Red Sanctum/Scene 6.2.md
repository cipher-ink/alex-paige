I was in my room for the better part of two weeks. I’d become a professional hermit, give or take a few days. Not exactly a vacation though. My days mostly consisted of crying, sleeping, and playing the Olympic sport of curling…into a ball of self loathing. I took home gold, naturally.
*Maybe if I’d been faster*, I thought, *or more powerful. Maybe if I hadn’t been born. Would he still be..*
Nope. Not going to finish that thought. My brain was already a broken record of “what-ifs”, and I needed to find the skip button. Desperately avoiding the bookshelf—which held unsolicited reminders of the worst day of may life—I scoured the room for another distraction. The desk offered pen and paper, which gave me exactly seven stick figure masterpieces before they started looking at me judgmentally. One of them even looked like that thing in my dream. That was enough to make me stop.
Eventually, I caved. The bookshelf won the staring contest, and with a sign, I climbed up onto the wobbly chair to reach the top row. The thing was practically groaning under the weight of Mr. Terrell’s “light reading”. It looked more like a library annex. Seriously, who needs twenty books per shelf? Did he think I had a magical device that would let me turn back time and repeat the same day over and over again just to read some books?
Too specific?
Well at least they weren’t musty old tomes. My first pick—a book with black and yellow stripes—was one I’d seen in bookstores before.
*Magic and the Weave for Dummies*.
Cute. All it needed was a snarky tag-line like “For those with zero clue and even less motivation”. I’d seen these guides in stores before (Algebra, anyone?), but I wasn’t expecting there to be one for magic. The absurdity almost made me laugh—almost.
The content was…dense. Terms flew at me faster than dodge-balls, smacking me right back to studying—which I’ve never been the best at. The book waves poetically about “The Weave”, describing it as some sort of cosmic tapestry by those lucky enough to have seen it. Threads here—connections there—blah blah blah. It sounded like I was reading an epic twelve-part fantasy rather than a textbook about something, ya know, real. I remembered back to the forest; had I seen this Weave? Honestly it felt more like a spiderweb than a tapestry, but who am I to argue with textbooks. I’m the dummy, after all.
Was me being able to see it why those people were after me? Was that why my dream visitor was interested in me?
I jotted down the questions on the metaphorical cork-board in my mind. Pretty soon, though, I’d need an actual one. Or two.
“Most Operators are able to sense the Weave,” I read, being given even more terms to think about, “But there are those with special abilities. Gifts given to them through the Weave. They are called *Weavers*.”
*Very original naming convention. And what the hell is an Operator?*
A glossary in the back of the book might have helped, but I was in no such luck. Oh I should just look things up on my phone? Didn’t have one. Never did. Would Google even let me search “What are operators” and “What is the weave”? Did the Veil’s  “no-magic-allowed” policy extend to the Internet?
That’s how my days went: reading, scribbling questions, rearranging papers into a big conspiracy map like you see in a TV show. I wasn’t leaving the room, but at least my brain had a hobby now. Mr. Terrell deserved a medal for “most patient elf of the ear”—still delivering food to my door, three times a day, without expecting a response. I knew he was giving me space, yet…I hated the silence. I couldn’t decide whether I like that he was giving me the space I needed, or hated that he wasn’t giving me any attention.
One night, I was limping back from the bathroom—my ankle wasn’t completely healed yet—but the house was eerily quiet. Usually, Mr. Terrell would be parked in front of the TV, doing his best impression of: “I want you to know I’m here, but I’m not going to be intrusive”. It was nice, but now only silence was greeting me in the hallway. No flickering lights, nor the muffled sounds of bas sitcom reruns.
“Mr. Terrell?” I called out, my voice almost echoing in the stillness.
Crickets. Not even a sarcastic reply. I hesitated in the hallway, my feet refusing to venture further into the shadowy living room. Curiosity was clawing at the back of my head, but my anxiety was keeping it in a headlock.
*Maybe you can find him with magic*, I thought. Sure, Alex, that sounded like a perfectly safe and rational idea. What could possibly go wrong.
Back in my room, I flopped into the bed and tried to remember what I’d read to this point. Cause I was that kind of nerd now, apparently. There was one book that talked about sensing *mana*—the European term for magic—and how it was always flowing in and out of the body naturally. Supposedly, you had to block that flow and control it if you wanted to actually use it. It recommended meditation.
Staying still; something I was *absolutely* great at.
I started with the basics, flexing and relaxing my toes while lying on the bed. Then moving to my feet, calves, thighs, all the way up to my face. I was told to “sink” into the floor, whatever that meant, but five rounds in and…nothing. Not even a *spark* of magical self-awareness.
“Ugh, you’ve gotta be kidding me.” I muttered.
I grabbed a book off the shelf and started flipping through the pages like a detective on a deadline. Finally, I landed on the one I was thinking of—a page that looked like It was from a biology textbook. It explained how different cultures approached “blocking the flow”. Europeans and their *mana* focused it in their chests. India focused *prana* in the small of their back, near the spine. And then good old Americans just called it magic and focused it on the tips of their fingers. I guess it was the easiest to control, which sounded about right for the US.
Most of this was going over my head—which was spinning like a dreidel by the way—but one thing stuck out. That phrase from the book:

>*Magic, in it’s most natural state, mirrors the flow of a river through the body. It is absorbed into the circulatory system. Once absorbed, it intertwines itself with the recipient’s blood, where it then circulates around the body. When channeled, it moves towards an exit point of the participant’s choosing. This is most likely to be a participant’s Crest, which acts as a focus for their energy.*

Within all that mumbo-jumbo, I think I had caught something. The way they described it flowing through your system, like a river. I had felt something like that before; the flower. It felt almost like…
“Breathing,” I whispered to myself.
I sat myself down, cross-legged in the middle of the room, to try again. This time, I imagined the flow like I’d done before. Like a current: in and out. I placed both my hands on the ground.
*Left in, right out.*
I repeated the phrase to myself as I focused on my breathing. I probably looked like a crazy person you’d see on the street, whispering to myself, but It was helping. Thinking of breathing in like the river flooding in, and breathing out like the river cascading down a hill and out of my body. An electric current following the least resistance.
Then, after what felt like forever, I felt it—a soft warmth starting at my fingertips, winding it’s way up my arm, swirling around through my chest, and down into my other hand. It was like feeling your heartbeat everywhere at once, my muscles twitching in response to wherever it flowed. I held on though, steadying my breath and keeping focus.
Well, I tried at least.
*Dad would think this was so cool.*
The thought hit like a sucker punch. My body jerked, and the warmth that was flowing nicely around my body suddenly tightened in the stomach. It was hot, like I’d just swallowed molten lava. My throat seized up, locking me in a silent scream as panic rushed in.
Air. I needed air.
I got up to my feet and grabbed the side of the bed for stability. My lungs began to burn, my knees buckled, and my vision began tunneling as vertigo yanked be backwards. I hit the floor with a loud *thud*, finally gasping as the tightness in my chest released.
I stayed on the floor for a bit, staring at the ceiling, savoring every shaky breath like I’d never take another.
“Not doing that again,” I wheezed, rolling over to my side.
I had been close—close to *something* at least. The flow was there, everything felt right. But I wasn’t able to control it.
I wished Dad were there. He would have given me a stupid Dad joke or two. He would have said something heartwarming and made me a nice cup of hot chocolate with a little something extra to take the edge off.
Or maybe even a hug.
My chest ached again. Something even *magic* couldn’t fix.
I grabbed my hoodie, and for the first time in weeks, left my room.
