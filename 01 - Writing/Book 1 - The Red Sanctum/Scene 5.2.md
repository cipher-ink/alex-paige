I jolted awake, my hands shooting out instinctively to protect myself from an attack I couldn’t see. My heart was pounding like a war drum. A startled yelp followed, and I whipped my head around to see Mr. Terrell leaning back, narrowly avoiding my hands.
“Oh geez,” he said, pressing a hand to his chest. “Don’t scare me like that, Alex.”
I didn’t have a clever comeback, just the ghost of a nightmare lingering behind my eyes, icy tendrils wrapping tightly around my chest. That dream wasn’t something I would forget *any* time soon.
I looked away from Mr. Terrell to the rest of the room, my breath shaky and terse. Wooden walls. A bed pressed up against a curtained window. A sparse collection of furniture—a nightstand, bookshelf, dresser, and a desk pushed into the corner opposite the bed. Dust danced in the beams of sunlight streaming through the curtains, which I hadn’t noticed were drawn open.
*Sunlight?*
“What time is it?” I asked, my voice raspy and dry.
“Around ten in the morning.”
I felt the blood wash from my face. His expression tightened and my pulse quickened. I ripped the covers off and swung my legs over the side of the bed. Pain shot through my foot like lightning as I stood, nearly crumpling under the weight. My ankle was bound tightly in bandages and the ache was sharp enough to make me want to scream. But I didn’t. I couldn’t. Instead, I limped to the door, determined and shaking.
“Alex, wait!” Mr. Terrell called, following me as I burst through.
My mind was racing too fast to care about the details—the woodsy cabin feel of the place, the warmth of sunlight, the faint creak of old floors beneath my feet, the oddly modern aesthetic. None of it mattered. I barreled into the living room, scanning wildly. I was searching. The cozy decor blurred into irrelevance as my search turned up nothing.
I spun on my good heel to Mr. Terrell, who was trailing nervously behind me.
“Where is he?”
He brought his hands up in surrender. A non-threatening gesture you would use against a rabid animal. His hesitation in answering only fanned the flames.
“Alex,” he said, trying to sound as calm as possible, “you need to calm down.”
“Tell. Me. Where. He. Is.” I demanded through gritted teeth, each word laced with enough poison to take out an elephant. The lights around me flickered ominously and the television behind me crackled to life, flipping through channels. The microwave dinged in the kitchen.
“You’ve been out for three days, Alex. You need to rest. You need to cal—”
He was cut off as the lights shattered above us. Shards of glass came raining down on the kitchen tile. My hands clenched into fists, trembling with this new realization.
“Three days?” I whispered, the words catching in my throat. “No. That’s not possible. I was just—”
I bolted for the door, ignoring Mr. Terrell’s protests. The sharp sting in my ankle now barely registered as I thrust myself into the outside. I stumbled into the open air, squinting against the brightness, my surroundings blurred and unfamiliar. A hill sloped down from the cabin, a dirt path winding lazily down and around a large forest.
Behind me, Mr. Terrell called softly, his tone too careful. Too fragile. In that tone that I loathed.
“Alex.”
I turned to him, the knot in my stomach twisting tighter with every word I couldn’t bring myself to say. My anger was gone. There was something different in its place. 
“Please,” I begged. “Tell me.”
His gaze dropped to the ground, silent. That silence spoke louder than anything he could have said. My chest became hollow, my legs buckling as I sank to my knees in the grass. It was taking all of my willpower to hold back the rain that was beginning to pool in my eyes, threatening to crash down in a thunderstorm at any moment.
Mr. Terrell came closer, reaching out a hand, but his eyes were still fixed on the ground.
I took it.
Not because I wanted to, but because I didn’t know what else to do. He guided me down the hill and along the snaking path of the road. I dragged my feet with every step, numb to any pain that would have come from my ankle. The rain begged to be let free, and I begged for it to go away.
The walk stretched on forever, each step heavier than the last. Mr. Terrell wasn’t talking. He wasn’t making jokes. I wanted him to joke. I wanted to hear how he’d had Julius Caesar for lunch while talking about his salad. I wanted to hear how George Washington’s apples didn’t fall far from the tree. His silence only added to the depth in my chest.
By the time we reached the bottom of the hill, I felt as empty as the void in my dream. He turned to look out off the edge of the path, his face filled with a sullen despair. I turned, following his gaze to a stone marker fifty feet off the road. It’s surface was rough and uneven. My heart continued to sink as my gaze landed on the crude etching along it’s surface.
I began to read.
And it began to rain.
It began to pour.
