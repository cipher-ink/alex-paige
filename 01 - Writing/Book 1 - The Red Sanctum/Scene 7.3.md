I was lucky; the rest of the *training* for the day was only a crash course in science—but ‘magically inclined’. Aka, science with some extra glitter. And by glitter, I mean fire.
“Evocation,” Mr. Terrell began in the lecture voice I’d head him use a million times, “is the art of harnessing raw energy and shaping it. There are many different sub-schools for specific things like fire, water, or even sound. The user then shapes them and adapts them to their specific needs.”
“Great, so I get to shoot fire from my hands?”
He nodded, a hint of amusement curling at the edges of his mouth. “Yes, you could. But it’s more about control. You shape the energy into what you need. While any magi can cast any spell, those closer to their affinity come more naturally.”
“Light,” I said.
“Yes. You have a special affinity for light. It’s rare, but not unheard of.”
“Rare like a unicorn? Or rare like a vending machine sometimes not giving you the snack you wanted?”
“Closer to the vending machine. It’s more like one in five thousand or so. We don’t keep specifics on the math, but you are the fourth at the school that I know of.”
“So like, my special power is…being a flashlight?” I deadpanned.
He chuckled, definitely amused at the thought, but shook his head. “It’s a little more than that. Let me show you.”
He held out his hand and muttered the word *flame*. His tattoo flared to life with a green glow, and a spark ignited within his palm, booming into a ball of fire the size of a grapefruit.
I flinched, my body going into a sudden *fight or flight* mode. I sharply inhaled, staring into the fire and remembering back to things that I didn’t want to think about.
*Calm down*, I wrestled, *It’s Mr. Terrell. It’s just Mr. Terrell.*
“Think of spells like baking a cake,” he explained, not noticing the panic in my eye. “Fire is the end product, but it takes ingredients, like…”
“Oxygen,” I answered, finally managing to get some of my own.
“Yes.”
“So back to science class, yeah?”
“Magic and science are two sides of the same coin,” he continued. With a flick of his wrist the flame swelled until it was the size of a small melon. I thought I saw it contort and flow like it was being whisked around by the air.
“If you add the right ingredient, you can change the result. Magic mostly acts as a catalyst.”
The fire then receded back to it’s original size before disapparating with a snap of his fingers.
“Cool, cool. So I add more oxygen to make the flame bigger.”
“And you have to maintain your concentration, or lose control of it.”
“So I’d burn myself.”
“That’s why control is key,” he said, leaning on the counter like he was getting into the real lecture now. “Every spell you cast can be altered if you focus. But that doesn’t mean it’s easy.”
“What about the whole ‘flame’ thing. Do I really have to say that every time? It sounds kind of lame.”
He answered with a smug grin, as though he was waiting for me to ask. Without another word, he reached out and grabbed hold of a mug that he’d pre-placed on the counter. It suddenly started to shake violently before cracking down the middle.
“Cool,” I said.
“As ‘lame’ as it sounds, incantations help you to visualize what you’re doing, making the spell easier to control. *Mend*.”
The mug shimmered and fused itself back together as though it had never been broken.
I guess I would have to deal with being lame.
“The easiest way to maintain a spell is to have it encoded onto a crest. That way the crest does all of the maintenance and you only have to provide it with magic. But we won’t be doing that yet.”
“No easy way out, is there?,” I muttered, already dreading the training to come.
“Not here.”
He moved the coffee table aside, clearing the soft, black-and-brown fur rug in the center of the living room. The room itself was cozy—probably too cozy for what I suspected was about to become my personal arena of failure. A fireplace with a TV mounted above, couches and love seats arranged like the seating for a spectator sport, an a fluffy rug that screamed *fancy bear pelt*.
Then he handed me a light bulb.
“What am I supposed to do with this? Make a wish?” I asked, staring at the light bulb like it might bite me.
“Light it up, of course. Use what you’ve practiced. Channel the energy around you into the bulb, and it should light. If you do it correctly.”
He plopped down onto the couch, and with zero fanfare, started flipping through channels.
“And if I don’t?”
“You’ll be picking glass out of that rug for the next hour,” he said casually, his eyes now unnaturally glued to the TV.
Oh great, I thought.
I settled in, cross-legged on the rug, clutching the bulb like a newborn puppy. *Focus*, I told myself. *Ignore the TV. Ignore Mr. Terrell being the winner of ‘most unhelpful half-elf of the year’. Just focus.*
But how was I supposed to focus when he spent the next hour cackling at a stupid alien documentary?
“Aliens built the pyramids?” he snorted. “Sure. And I’m the king of Mars.”
I glared at him with all the sarcasm I could muster, trying not to laugh myself. This was most definitely part of my training—a distraction test disguised as bad TV.
I turned away, squeezing my eyes shut to block him out and started on the breathing exercises from the book. Inhale, exhale. Focus on the flow, like water through a stream. I imagined the energy gathering, swirling in my core, before flowing out and into my hands.
At first, it was subtle—just a faint warmth spreading through my fingertips. Then I heard it: a soft, rushing sound like a creek winding through the woods. I opened my eyes. The bulb was glowing faintly, the filament beginning to shimmer with the tiniest of embers.
“Good,” Mr. Terrell said. It took all my focus not to jump—he had moved to lean against the windowsill without me noticing. “Keep going. Push the flow, but stay in control. It’s not about strength; it’s about finesse. Think of it like a muscle. Flex it.”
Easy for him to say.
I took another breath, visualizing the energy. I flexed my whole body trying to push out the energy, but it felt like the only thing I was going to be pushing out was a bunch of *gas*. I could hear static coming from the TV.
“You’re pushing to hard,” Mr. Terrell warned. “Magic is spiritual, not physical. It’s YOU that makes it physical.”
*Right*, I thought. *Spiritual. Zen and stuff.*
I went back to my breathing, drawing back to an old memory of a documentary I’d watched with my Dad. These swordsmiths in Japan would forge legendary katanas by secluding themselves in the mountains for days at a time. I remembered the scene of them bellowing the flames, feeding the air into the fire—slowly, with purpose.
I tried to mirror the rhythm with my breathing. In. Out. Focus.
The bulb began to flare up. Dull at first, then growing steadily until it lit the room like a miniature sun.
“Mr. Terrell, it’s—”
“Alex, don’t lose—”
*Pop!*
The bulb exploded in a shower of glass. The room plunged into darkness as I could hear the sparks of the breakers blowing.
“Well,” I said, my voice shaking. “That could have gone better.”
“Please tell me you didn’t fry the wiring.”
“Is that even possible?”
There wasn’t a response other than the sound of Mr. Terrell going outside to check the breaker.
*It’s magic, Alex. Anything is possible.*
While it didn’t take long for Mr. Terrell to get the power back on, it *did* take two hours to get all the glass out of the rug. And my skin. Turns out, small shards of glass are really good at playing hide and seek. I had to take a shower to make sure I had gotten it all since apparently there wasn’t a “Mr. Clean” magic spell.
The hot water stung, but I was stuck in a high at the moment. For the first time I’d *done* it. Deliberately controlled my power—my *mom’s* power. Sure, I lost control in the end and, you know, exploded a light bulb in my face. But for those few moments? It felt like I could have done anything.
I stepped back into the living room with fresh clothes and a towel to dry the wet mop that was my hair.
“So,” I said, throwing myself onto the couch, “Do I get to watch TV now? Or am I going to be exploding some more stuff? Cause it’s already, like, nine.”
“I’m proud of you, Alex.” Mr. Terrell responded, having moved from the kitchen back to the living room to sit next to me.
I grinned, throwing some finger guns in his direction. “Thanks, me too.”
He didn’t roll his eyes or groan at the cringe like I’d expected him to. Instead, he just kept smiling with that same infectiously supportive smile he would give someone when they answered a pop-quiz question correctly.
“I mean it,” he said. “I’m surprised at how quickly you pick all of this up. You’re certainly doing it faster than I did.”
“Oh come on,” I said, rolling my eyes. “I’m sure you were *fine*.”
He laughed—a real, honest laugh that caught me off guard. “Oh no. I was a terrible student.”
“Mr. Terrell? *Terrible*? No way.”
“Way.”
I perked up. “Forget TV. I need details. Now. Spill it.”
And just like that, the night turned into “Storytime with Mr. Terrell”. Turns out, he wasn’t always the wise, no-nonsense teacher I knew. He’d been a troublemaker. Skipping classes, flirting with girls—apparently, he had been *that guy* back in the day.
My jaw was past the couch and on the floor. The idea of Mr. Terrell being anything other than a goody-two-shoes? Wild. But honestly? It gave me hope. If he could be a screw-up and still turn into someone that people actually trusted, maybe I had a shot at not being a walking disaster myself.
I hung on his every word, but somewhere between his stories of magical mischief and near-expulsions, sleep caught up with me.
I didn’t even realize that I’d nodded off.
