The rest of the car ride was unnervingly quiet. Dad had broken it down for me—The Sanctum wasn’t exactly “Dad-friendly”. Only people with magic were allowed, and as much as I thought my Dad was magical, the school would say otherwise. I tried to argue—something about how the rules were meant to be bent, right?—but my half-baked ideas couldn’t even convince *me*. I had no clue how this magic stuff worked, and my brain had already been doing an entire circus routine all day just to keep up with everything that happened.
Ten more minutes of awkward silence went by before Dad slowed the car, flicking on the brights and squinting at the side of the road like he’d lost a contact or something. The left side of the road was nothing but trees, while the right side featured a thrilling five-foot buffer of grass before—surprise—more trees. Every few miles there would be a clearing or something, but Dad didn’t seem interested in those.
“There,” he finally muttered.
I followed his gaze to a mile marker on the side of the road with the label ‘1922’. Odd for a mile marker to have four digits, but was that it? 
“You mean…the mile marker? That’s where we’ve been going?” I asked.
“Yes.”
Oh cool, no further *explanation needed then*.
Dad parked the car and hopped out, wincing as his burned shoulder smacked the door frame. I scrambled after him—at least after telling my ankle to stop throwing another tantrum. He offered a hand, and we limped our way over to the marker, but there was…nothing. Just more trees, thick and tangled up like they were competing to see who could grow in the most wild fashion.
“What now?” I asked, my arm draped over my Dad’s shoulder.
“I don’t know.”
“What do you mean, ‘I don’t know’? You brought us here.”
“Well, your teacher said you’d know what to do.”
“Oh, well of course he did. Classic Mr. Terrell.” I said, rolling my eyes so hard they almost got stuck in the back of my head. “He always expects me to just FIND the answer like it’s in my…”
I trailed off, remembering his words.
“…pocket.”
I reached into the left side jacket pocket and brought out the culprit. My fingers closed around the stone carving he had given to me at the museum. A griffin, I now remembered. Its carved wings and lion body felt solid and lifeless.
Until they weren’t.
The statue shuddered in my palm, the runes etched along its neck beginning to glow a faint purple. Its wings snapped open, stretching like a cat that had just woken up from a long nap. It craned its tiny eagle head to look at me, and I swear, it was judging me with those eyes that said ‘Ugh, I have to help *you*?’. It then flapped its wings, floated into the air—like that was normal for a statue—and circled Dad and me a few times. It then darted off into the woods.
“I guess we’re supposed to follow?” I pondered.
Dad shrugged his shoulders as we made our way in.
The glowing collar on the griffin made it easy to track, bobbing up and down like a magical firefly as it darted between the trees. I kept up okay, but Dad wasn’t so lucky—roots seemed magnetically attracted to his feet. Every few seconds, there was a new curse word added to my mental tally.
“It’d be great if there was, oh I don’t know, a *path* or something,” I muttered.
And just like that, the forest shifted. Okay, well maybe not literally, but it felt as though the trees around us were shifting, suddenly leading us to a narrow trail snaking through the underbrush like it had been there all along.
*Magic*, I scoffed to myself.
As we continued the walk, I built up the courage to ask:
“What are you going to do? You’re sure they won’t let you stay even for a little bit?”
“I’m not sure,” he sighed, “Your Mom told me that her people—the wizards—aren’t too fond of us ‘Laymans’. Some of them are nice to us, but not all.”
“Laymans,” I repeated. Mr. Terrell had used the word before, but when Dad said it, it felt more like it was made to be an insult.
“Be careful, Al. She said that the school would be safe from those people who are after you, but I’m your Dad. I’m going to worry.”
I let out a small scoff, trying to act like I was calm and composed—while being anything but.
“Yeah, I’ll figure it out.”
I was lying through my teeth, obviously. If normal schools had hierarchies, what would a school for *magic* have? Jocks with fire powers? Mean girls who could *literally* curse you? I wasn’t sure that I was prepared for that. As if reading my mind, Dad chimed:
“I’m sure the *Soda Witch* will be perfectly fine.”
“Wow,” I snorted—my Dad the comedian, everybody—and smacked him on the shoulder. “Thanks for the vote of confidence.”
We finally caught up to the griffin, who had stopped at a small clearing in the trees. It wasn’t much—just a five-foot clearing, some shale cliffs…oh and a mountain that appeared out of nowhere. Its peak disappeared above us, piercing the clouds like it was Mt. Olympus or something. How had I not noticed it on the way here? *Magic*, obviously.
The griffin perched itself on my shoulder, ruffling its stone feathers as if trying to say: I’ve been waiting for *SO* long. Then, out of nowhere, it jabbed me in the neck with its beak.
“Dude! Seriously?” I glared, smacking it away from my shoulder and holding my neck. It fluttered over to the cliff face and started pecking it too, turning to me with a pointed look.
“What? It’s a rock.”
If stone griffins could roll their eyes, I think It would have. It flew back to me, circling my head too many times and making an odd scratching sound that I think was supposed to be chirping.
“Fine! Fine!” I said, walking over to the cliff. I ran my hand over the rock, which was cool to the touch. It went up over a hundred feet, and some parts were jagged, but it just looked like a rock. Just as I was about to turn and curse Mr. Terrell for giving me a faulty griffin, Dad got my attention.
“Hey, Al, over here.”
Dad was fifty feet away, pointing at a weird indentation on the rock. He had found a small hole dug into the cliff face. The cuts were way too perfect for it to be natural, so I  bent down and took a look. It was thin, with an odd shape that I couldn’t recognize. That was until the griffin once again pecked me in the neck.
“If you do that again, I’m…”
I squinted my eyes at the griffin, reaching up to grab it. It then became lifeless in my hand, returning to its original state. I turned the griffin so that it was facing me and placed it at the edge of the hole. It clicked into place.
The runes around its neck flared to life and the cliff face began to glow. Lines of magic began to spread out from the griffin and into the rock like cracks in a window pane. They came together to form an archway with engravings of different symbols that I wasn’t able to read. Some of them looked more like pictures of creatures than symbols, but as the magic flowed through they began to glow one by one. They were all connected together, like a spider web.
Then the archway lit up. The inside began to swirl with colors—a cosmic nebula on the other side. Purples, blues, and golds shimmered in the vast expanse beyond like an ocean of celestial bodies that stretched out to infinity.
Then it changed. I was looking into the blurry image of a circular room with marble floors and large columns reaching up beyond the view of the portal. Hints of red and gold highlighted the edges of the room, which was lit by sconces on the column. It looked like the fanciest ballroom you’d ever seen in your life—and that was just the entryway.
Before I knew it, I had placed my hand against the portal. More out of curiosity than wanting to go. My hand began to pass through, but it felt like I was sticking my hand through Jello. The portal wrapped around my hand, yielding to the pressure while also remaining resilient. I wiggled my fingers and watched the waves ripple across the portal like a lake as the resistance on my hand intensified. I got the sense that It didn’t want me half-way through the portal. It was either in or out, so I pulled back.
“Fancy,” my Dad said, his eyes scanning the portal like he was sizing up a work of art or a snazzy car. He reached out and brushed the edge of the archway, his expression caught somewhere between awe and confusion. “So this is the Sanctum? Doesn’t look much like a school to me.”
“I’m pretty sure the school is *through* the portal, Dad.” I chuckled. The laugh got caught in my throat as the thought suddenly dawned on me. I looked at the portal—a painting come to life—but the sight of it only twisted my stomach.
“I guess this Is where I’m supposed to go through.”
I felt Dad’s hand on my shoulder, grounding me as I stared into the swirling lights. I didn’t want to turn around, didn’t want to see the goodbye in his eyes. But I did. His face, lined with exhaustion and pain, still held that familiar warmth—the kind that always told me everything would be okay, even when I knew it wouldn’t be.
“Are you okay?” he asked.
“Not by a long shot.”
He brought me in for a hug, wincing at the movement but not letting that stop him. His arms wrapped tightly around me, and I buried my face in his flannel. It was taking all the willpower I had to stop the rain from falling down my cheeks.
“I don’t want to say goodbye,” I said, my voice breaking on the last word. “Please don’t make me say it.”
“This isn’t goodbye, Al.”
He brushed my hair back and away from my face as I sniffled, holding back the dam for as long as I could. I bet I looked like one of those mucus monsters from the allergy medicine commercials.
“Instead, you’re supposed to say ‘See you later, alligator!’, and then I say ‘In a while, corrodible’.”
“It’s not the same.”
He smiled a lopsided grin that had always meant he was either about to drop a signature Dad joke or be the corniest man on the planet. Probably both.
“It is, ‘cause I’m not going anywhere. I’ll always be here,” He tapped a finger to my temple, and then to my chest. “And here. You can’t get rid of me that easily, Al.”
“Dad, stop.” I tried not to laugh, but it came out as a wet, hiccuping sound. “You’re so cheesy.”
“It’s my job. And you love it.”
“No! I…yeah. I do.”
I hugged him again. I didn’t want to go.
I didn’t want to say it.
“Hey, Al.”
I looked up and into my Dad’s eyes. They were warm; nurturing. I could see the struggle in his face. He was staying strong. For me.
“Listen to me. You’re gonna be amazing in there. Look, I know it’s scary, and I know you don’t want to do this right now, but I believe in you. It’s just another school, right? You’ve been to plenty of those.”
“Dad…”
“Let me finish,” he said, holding up a hand. “You’re gonna make friends. You’re gonna learn all kinds of incredible things. And one day, you’re gonna change the world. Like your mother.”
“What is that supposed to mean?”
“Well she changed *my* world, didn’t she? And my world is the one that counts!”
I laughed, cried, and hiccupped all at the same time, my emotions at their peak. I clung to him again, my arms wrapping tightly around his waist.
“I love you.”
“I love you too, Al.”
The rain began to pour from my eyes. I stepped back and turned towards the portal. My hand hovered just in front of the swirling light. There was a cool breeze coming off of it and against my skin. I wiped away as much as I could before turning back to him with the most painful smile that I thought I’d ever made.
“See you later, alligator.”
He responded with that same lopsided grin of his.
“In a while, croc—”
We were interrupted by an explosion.
