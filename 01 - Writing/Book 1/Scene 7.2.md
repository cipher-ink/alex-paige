The training *started* with a three mile run. Scratch that—six miles, because we had to run there *and* back. Mr. Terrell led me down winding paths alongside the forest. Normally, I would be all about soaking in the beauty of the trees and the soft breeze, but It was hard to appreciate nature when I was busy wheezing like a dying accordion.
We passed Dad along the way, the black-and-white marble of the stone standing out like a brooding sentinel among the green hills. It tugged at something deep in my chest, but I pushed it down and focused on keeping my legs moving.
By the time we made it back to the cabin, I was bent over, hands on my knees, gasping for air.
“I don’t think I’ve ever run this much in my life, ” I panted, swearing to never talk walking for granted again.
“Well,” Mr. Terrell said without even a drop of sweat on him, “We’re doing this every morning. You’ll get used to it.”
I groaned and flopped to the ground in protest.
“Used to it?!” I mumbled to the dirt, “Sure. Right after I become a morning person.”
After a short break—which wasn’t nearly long enough—we made our way back to his cabin. The house itself was situated on the right side of the path at the base of a small hill. Beyond the path and behind the house was a clearing with that looked like a playing field carved into the grass. It was around the size of a soccer field, but instead of goal zones, there were these evenly spaced white squares on each side and a wide-open area in the middle.
“Do wizards play sports?”
“Yeah. This field is for *Pillar*,” he stated, as if that really explained anything.
“Color me surprised.”
“What, did you think we spent all of our time indoors reading ‘musty old tomes’?”
“…maybe.”
That got a hearty chuckle from him. “So we just aren’t allowed to have fun?”
“It was a working theory.” I shrugged.
He led me over to a small wooden deck on the side of the field, complete with chairs (All matching, gross.), a grill, and a storage shed. The vibe screamed “backyard BBQ” more than “magical training ground”. From the shed, he pulled out four cloth-covered spheres and set them on the table with a thud.
“Are we playing Pillar?”
“Huh? No,” he said, unwrapping the spheres. “You’d need a Crest for that.”
Ah, Crests—the tattoo marks that I’d seen on Mr. Terrell’s arm and the cowboy mannequin a the museum. Mr. Terrell’s looked like someone had combined intricate circuit board designs with elegant runes, all flowing together in a circular pattern that was oddly mesmerizing.
“Do all Crests look like that?”
“Not at all. Each one is unique,” he said, pulling the last cloth away to reveal the spheres. They were made of crystal, each a different color: orange, blue, violet, and green. “Crests CAN be passed down through generations, but we won’t get into that yet.”
*Great, more homework*. I thought, turning my attention to the spheres.
The orange one looked like a thunderstorm trapped in glass, lightning periodically flashing inside. The blue one shimmered, shifting between opaque and translucent like a heat mirage. The violet one swirled with mist, faint shapes—faces, maybe—would emerge from the clouds and then vanish as quickly as they appeared. The green one had a kaleidoscope effect, it’s fragmented surface catching the light in a rainbow.
“These,” Mr. Terrell said with a dramatic flair of his hands, “are Truth Orbs. They’re used to determine someone’s affinity for magic.”
“So like, I touch the orb and it glows?”
“Exactly,” he replied, un-fazed by my sarcastic tone. “Do you remember the four classes of magic?”
I hesitated, thinking back to my reading.
“Evocation, Illusion, Conjuration, and…”
“Enchantment.”
“I was getting there!”
“Right,” he said. “Well, if you have an affinity for one of the schools, the Truth Orbs will react to your touch.”
To demonstrate, he placed his hands on each orb, starting with the orange one. Nothing happened as he went down the line, until he got to the green one. It lit up, it’s glow intensifying before it turned into solid silver. He tapped it with his keys, producing a metallic clink.
“My affinity is Enchantment,” he explained. “I can change the structure of inorganic material—as long as I know it’s chemical composition.”
“And yet you were teaching history.” I quipped.
“Just because I know something doesn’t mean I enjoy it,” he shot back with a grin.
“Sure, sure. But what about living things?”
His smile faltered, if only for a moment.
“It’s…possible. But its very difficult and…frowned upon. It can have…messy consequences.”
“Gross,” I muttered.
“Very. Anyway, you’re turn.” He gestured to the orbs.
I approached the table, deciding the order in which I would grab them, before a thought crossed my mind.
“Has anyone ever had more than one affinity? Please tell me I’m not about to become a ‘chosen one’ or something.”
“Not that I’ve ever heard of,” he said. “It’s theoretically impossible.”
“Right then,” I said, steeling myself. “Let’s do this.”
One by one, I touched the orbs. I started with the violet one, since purple was my favorite. The mist swirled around like it was staring at me but ultimately did nothing. One down.
My hand touched the blue one, but then my hand just passed through it. It felt like I was moving my hand through water. Second one down.
I thought about the green one as I reached out. Maybe I would have the same magic as Mr. Terrell. That would be cool, right? Well, It didn’t do anything.
“Last one,” Mr. Terrell said.
I stared at the orange orb, it’s stormy interior crackling with all the attitude of a teenager. My hand trembled as I reached for it. What if it didn’t react? What If I didn’t have any affinity at all? Would that mean that everything we’d done so far—everything that I lost—was for nothing? I looked back at Mr. Terrell for support—
He was giving me two enthusiastic thumbs up, his grin so wide that it should have been on a cartoon. I burst into laughter, the tension melting away.
“What?” he asked, genuinely sounding puzzled.
“Nothing, nothing,” I gasped between giggles. “I’m just over here freaking out, and here you look like a Dad sending his kid off to kindergarten. You’ve even got the shoes.”
“What’s wrong with New Balance?” he said, crossing his arms in mock-offense.
“Nothing. It’s what my Dad wore.”
His smile dropped.
“Alex, I-I didn’t mean to…I’m not trying to—”
“It’s okay, Mr. Terrell. I appreciate it. I do. Thank you.”
He went through a series of looks that said: thinking of what to say, can’t find anything to say, now what do I do? Instead of saying anything, he gave another thumbs up.
I placed my hand on the orange orb.
I really should have brought shades, cause the sphere lit up brighter than a white screen during the middle of the night. Within seconds, my hand began to feel hot. When it started to feel like I was holding my hand on a stove top, I yanked it back. The light eventually faded, but the orange orb was still glowing like molten metal, the storm inside now resembling waves of plasma sloshing around.
I turned to Mr. Terrell, only to find him wearing a pear of sunglasses.
“Did you know that was going to happen?” I asked, blinking my eyes as they re-adjusted to normal lighting.
“I had an idea,” he said casually.
“Thanks for the warning,” I shot back.
“I brought you a pair,” he said, pulling another set of sunglasses from his pocket, “but I forgot to give them to you.”
I flopped into a chair, suddenly feeling exhausted. Whatever just happened had zapped out all of my energy and now I felt like I had a bad Wi-Fi connection.
“So what does that mean,” I asked. “What kind of magic do I have?”
“Evocation,” he said, sitting down next to me and gazing into the forest. From this vantage point I could see that it stretched endlessly south, a blanket of green so dense it looked painted. To the north was another hill, higher than ours, with a trail leading to the top. I made a mental note to check it out later—assuming I survived all this “training”.
“So what is Evocation, really?” I asked.
“Why don’t you tell me what your books said.”
“Uh…something about creating things?”
“Yes, that’s almost right. To be more precise, evocation produces tangible, physical things. Elements, air, sound—things that you can interact with. It’s the most versatile and widely used school of magic.”
“I should they were called classes.”
“Oh, right.” He said, giving an apologetic shrug. “You’ll find that the professors at the Sanctum tend to use the older magic terms. So ‘school’ instead of ‘class’.”
“Why change it?”
“Why does language evolve? Kids these days don’t talk like I did growing up. And neither will their kids. Words change over time.”
“Okay, boomer,” I muttered.
He chuckled but kept going.
“Operators today don’t study magic the way we used to. Calling them Magi would be a generous title. Casting a spell on a Crest takes about as much effort as pulling the trigger and saying it’s incantation. It’s all—”
He must have caught my glazed-over stare because he stopped mid-lecture.
“Sorry,” he said with a sheepish grin, “I get a little heated about this. Magic tech has made things a lot easier, and the old ways are fading away.”
“You sound like a geezer when you talk about history.”
“That’s because I’m trying to preserve it,” he said, leaning back into his chair. His expression shifted, his hazel eyes scanning the sky. For a moment he looked older—like someone carrying the weight of something slipping through his grasp.
“Is that why you work for the Sanctum?”
He nodded. “I’m the Head of Preservation. I digitize ancient magical records to make sure they’re never lost. Of course, I’m not really a ‘head’ of anyone since it’s just me.”
He rubbed the bridge of his nose and I wanted to say something comforting. Nothing was coming to mind. Instead, I just sat there, watching him wrestle with his thoughts. It his me then—Mr. Terrell had his own life before all of this. Before me. He had his own problems. His own dreams.
“I’m sorry your stuck with me,” I muttered.
“Absolutely not,” he snapped, his posture straightening. His expression turned sharp, pulling me back to the museum. “Don’t say that about yourself. I’m not stuck with you.”
“I mean, you kind of are,” I joked, throwing some fingers guns to diffuse the situation. They didn’t work.
“I asked to help you, Alex,” he said firmly. “I wanted to. I pleaded with the headmaster to get you the materials and let you attend.”
“Okay,” I mumbled, my eyes dropping to the table. I didn’t really believe the stuff I was saying, but it’s what my brain fed to my mouth. Stupid intrusive thoughts ruining all my happy moments.
After an awkward silence the size of the great lakes, Mr. Terrell stood and ruffled my hair.
“Hey!”
“Hay is for horses,” he said, smirking. “Let’s grab some lunch, okay?”
My stomach grumbled in agreement.
“Sure,” I said, getting up to follow. “Sounds like a plan.”
“And then more *training*.”
My stomach decidedly didn’t like that part.
