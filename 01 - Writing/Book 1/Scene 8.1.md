There’s always this weird calm before the storm. You know the one I’m talking about--the wind stills, the clouds hover, and everything just...waits.
That’s what today felt like. Like the world was holding its breath. Like tomorrow, everything would be different.
Again.
I had climbed up to my usual perch in these past two months; there was this small flat area around the slanted roof that I could sit on comfortably if I crossed my legs. After a long day of running, reading, and exploding light bulbs, It was nice to come up here with a blanket and a nice mug of hot chocolate to look at the sky.
Tonight wasn’t any different.
I sipped my cocoa; the good kind. Not that store bought powder, but the thick, real-deal kind that Mr. Terrell made whenever he caught me staring into space for too long. It was his way of asking “Are you okay?” without actually having to ask anything and risk a snarky deflection.
Truthfully...I wasn’t sure. About tomorrow. About any of this.
There was supposed to be two months. Two months of something normal before I was supposed to start this whole new chapter of my life. Something grand and ambitious, treading in my mother’s footsteps. At least that’s how it was always described. But those two months went by too fast. I was so focused on everything; the reading, training, more reading, and blowing up lightbulbs. I probably went through and entire hardware store’s supply. The one thing he never had me do was actually cast a spell. It was more theory than practice. I’d had more terms thrown at me than the last three years of school combined--notes on magic, the weave, crest design theory, runic scripting, magical history, enchantments, the properties of various metals, and at least five pages of notes about something called a Trial.
And I missed it already.
I would have given almost anything to have another two months of sitting in the cabin. Sitting in peace.
But there wasn’t going to be such luck. Everything would change tomorrow.
“The sky looks good tonight.”
I jumped, cocoa sloshing over the rim of my mug and onto the blanket.
“Mother--!” I caught myself from finishing the sentence before turning around. “Could you *not* sneak up on people mid emotional flashback? I was having a very dramatic moment over here.”
Mr. Terrell gave one of his trademark “Did I do that?” shrugs.
“You said you wanted to be left alone with your thoughts. You *didn’t* say anything about me silently joining them.”
I glared at him over my cup, which was now only half full and a lot less cozy. I didn’t know how to feel about him being able to keep up with my remarks.
He moved slowly, settling down next to me with an ease that made the roof feel more like a couch than a slanted death trap. Of course *he* wasn’t worried about falling. He probably had enchanted boots or something with anti-slip spells.
“I figured I’d find you up here,” he said, watching the sky. “Last night before classes. Big day tomorrow.”
“Yeah,” I gargled in the middle of a sip. “Huge. Life-changing. Monumental.”
“Terrifying?”
“Bingo.”
He nodded, probably having expected that answer, and looked back towards the sky, letting a soft silence fall over the two of us. It wasn’t awkward--it never was with Mr. Terrell. He had this knack for being quiet *with* you rather than *at* you. Like he was giving you space, but still holding close in case you fell.
I pulled the blanket tighter around my shoulders and said, “Do you think I’ll get used to it?”
“To what?”
I gestured vaguely at the horizon. “All of this. Magic. Spells. Wizards. Cat people.”
*Weird void monsters offering you tea*, I thought.
“I don’t know,” he replied.
“Very encouraging.”
“Well,” he continued, still facing the sky, “I was born in this life. I’ve always known about magic. And the people you’re going to school with are probably the same. It’s rough to have been thrust into this kind of situation.”
My cocoa was now lukewarm.
“But you’ve done well, Alex,” he said. “Better than you have any right to have, really, given...everything.”
No clarification needed there, I think. We’d gone over everything that happened more times than I could count. I’d aired out all of my frustrations over these past two months. Did that mean I was cured? Absolutely not. But I wasn’t ready to give up. Not yet.
“Yeah, well,” I said, trying to not get all *squishy-feely* about it. “Maybe I’m just really good at faking it.”
He turned to look at me now, and it wasn’t with the expression of my teacher, nor the gym coach who’d made me run six miles every day.
It was of a friend. A friend who *understood*.
“You’re not faking it, Alex,” he said simply. “You’re surviving. There’s a difference.”
I didn’t have a snarky reply, so I said nothing. Instead, I looked up to the stars. Without any sort of city around, they shone brightly in the sky. They shimmered and twinkled like they were listening to me. I wanted to whisper something. To ask a question.
“Where is she?”
I hadn’t meant to say it aloud, but it came out as a soft whisper.
“You’ll find her.” Mr. Terrell replied softly.
What followed was another minute of silence. I didn’t know what to say. I’d told him about my Mom and me wanting to go search for her, but I had no idea where to start. All I’d had was a vague dream about some weird valley or whatever. There wasn’t a road sign that said “You are Here” in my dreams.
Mr. Terrell stood, brushing the nonexistent dust from his pants.
“Let’s head down. You’ll want to be rested for tomorrow. The fifth year syllabus is...dense.”
“Define, ‘dense’,” I said, uncurling my legs to stand.
“Let’s just say you might want another notebook.”
I groaned, but followed Mr. Terrell down from the roof.
Because tomorrow, the storm was coming.
And I was going to be in the middle of it.
