# The Vampire of Lucerine

*A Folk Tale of the Northern Provinces, circa 1843*

---

In the age when the old roads still ran silver with moonlight, there stood a quiet town called Lucerine, nestled between the foothills and the frostbitten pines. Its people were craftsmen and farmers, simple folk who rose with the sun and barred their doors when the stars came out. For Lucerine had a secret that only the night kept truly hidden.

They called it the Vampire.

It came first as a shadow stretched too long across the cobblestones. Then came the screams. Folk would find their neighbors in the morning, pale as winter linen, every drop of life drained from their veins. Upon their necks were two small holes, no wider than a sewing needle, neat and terrible. No blood stained the ground. No wound bled. It was as if the body had been wrung dry from within, leaving nothing but a hollow shell and a room that smelled of copper and cold magic.

Those who glimpsed the beast and lived spoke of grey, leathery skin that seemed to absorb the lamplight. Eyes that burned like coals plucked from a dying fire. Limbs elongated and twisted, moving with a skittering grace that made the stomach turn. It wore no clothes, needed none—its very flesh seemed armored by whatever dark bargain had made it.

But bargains, as the wise know, are rarely made by monsters alone.

---

The creature that haunted Lucerine was not born in any den or ditch. It had once been a man. The brother of Duchess Lenora Venn, to be precise—a gentleman of refined tastes and gentle bearing, husband to a soft-spoken merchant's daughter, father to a little girl with hair the color of wheat and a laugh like bells. His name had been Alaric, and by all accounts, he had been kind.

The Venn family, however, carried a stain older than their titles.

They called it the Sanguine Curse, though no one outside the bloodline knew it by any name at all. It was a rot that slept in the marrow, generation after generation, waiting for the moment when the body's natural magic bloomed too bright. For in that world—and perhaps in ours—the blood is more than water. The circulatory system is the highway upon which magic travels, the river current that carries power to every finger and thought. And blood, being the vessel of that current, is the finest catalyst known to the old arts.

When Alaric's curse awakened, it did not merely change his shape. It rewrote his hunger.

The power held in another's blood became an intoxicant he could not refuse. By drinking deep, he did not simply take life—he stole the magic that flowed within it, absorbing the abilities of those he fed upon like a sponge taking on water. A mage of fire would find his flames dimmed forever. A healer would wake with her gifts guttered out. And Alaric would walk away stronger, faster, more terrible than before, the stolen power knitting itself into his cursed flesh.

He fought it. God help him, he fought it.

When the first moon passed and he woke with blood on his lips and no memory of the night, Alaric begged his sister to lock him away. The Duchess, torn between duty and love, sealed him in the deepest cellar of the keep, behind iron doors and warding glyphs. But a curse that knows its host's mind is a clever thing. Alaric would barricade himself one evening, weeping apologies to his wife and clutching his daughter's doll, and by midnight the hunger would peel him open from within. The thing that climbed out of that cellar was not Alaric anymore. It was the Vampire.

And Lucerine paid the price.

---

For three winters, the town lived in terror. No one walked alone after dark. Windows were painted black to hide the candlelight. Mothers told their children that the Vampire did not exist, even as they checked the locks twice and slept with knives beneath their pillows. The tavern emptied by sundown. The night watch quit in droves, for what good is a torch against a thing that drinks power itself?

The Duchess tried to govern as if nothing were wrong, but the whispers followed her. *Her brother. Her blood. Her curse.* She hid Alaric's wife and daughter in the eastern wing, protected by guards who knew the truth and kept it locked behind their teeth. Little Sarah grew up asking why Papa could not come out to play, and her mother wept into her hands where no one could see.

Then came the knight.

His name is lost to history—some say it was Aldric, others swear it was something foreign and sharp-edged. He arrived on a roan horse with armor that had seen southern deserts and eastern seas, bearing a sword that hummed with enchantment. He had heard of the Lucerine Beast in a tavern three towns over, and he had come to do what local courage could not.

The knight did not wait for the creature to come to him. He studied the patterns of the attacks, mapped the streets where bodies were found, and noticed what others had missed: the trail always led back to the keep.

On the fourth night, he did not patrol the town. He scaled the cliffs behind the Venn estate and slipped through a servants' entrance left unwarded in the Duchess's grief. Down he went, past the wine cellars and the grain stores, into the dark beneath the dark, where the air grew thick with the smell of old magic and desperation.

What he found there would haunt him until his own dying day.

The Duchess was waiting for him. Not with guards, but with the truth laid bare. She told him of the Sanguine Curse, of generations of her family who had managed to hide their affliction behind wealth and status, feeding only from willing servants and prisoners who vanished from the records. She told him of Alaric's struggle, of the nights he chained himself with silver only to break them, of the love he still held for his family even as the hunger scraped him hollow. She showed him the alchemical journals, the crude attempts at a cure written in languages no living scholar could read. And she showed him the cellar door, shaking on its hinges, as something behind it scented the knight's powerful blood and began to scream.

The knight understood, then, that he had not been hunting a monster. He had been hunting a disease made flesh.

But understanding and mercy are not always the same thing.

When the door shattered and the Vampire emerged—grey-skinned and crimson-eyed and ravenous—the knight met it with his sword. The battle was terrible and brief. The creature had stolen so much magic by then that it wielded powers no single beast should possess: flames that burned black, wind that cut like glass, concussive force that cracked the ancient stone. But the knight was skilled, and his blade was old, and in the end he struck true.

The Vampire fell.

As the light left its red eyes, the grey skin seemed to soften, the limbs shrinking back toward something almost human. For a moment, the knight swore he saw a man's face beneath the monstrous mask—a face lined with relief as much as pain. Then it was still.

Alaric Venn was dead.

---

The knight brought his news to the Lord Regent who oversaw the province, and the truth spread like plague itself. The Venn bloodline had been cursed for centuries, hiding their affliction behind titles and stone walls, letting common folk die to feed their secret hunger. The scandal was enormous. The outrage, absolute.

Duchess Lenora was stripped of her titles. The family was banished from the keep they had held for three hundred years, cast out with nothing but the clothes on their backs and the curse in their veins. The Lord Regent declared that no Venn would ever again hold land or office in his domain, for a bloodline so tainted could not be trusted with power over others.

The knight received a medal he never wore. He left Lucerine by the same road he had entered, and some say he threw his enchanted sword into a river rather than keep a blade that had killed a man who only wanted to be locked away.

As for the town itself, the killings stopped. The night watch returned. Windows were unshuttered, and children played in the streets after sunset. Lucerine healed, as towns do.

But the curse was not ended. Only displaced.

The Venn family scattered to the winds, taking their secret with them, breeding in shadow and poverty, waiting for the next generation's blood to sing too loud. Somewhere, in some forgotten cellar, another brother or sister or nephew is even now begging to be locked away. Another family hides the truth behind locked doors and terrified loyalties. And somewhere, another town smells of copper and cold magic on the morning after a full moon.

For the Sanguine Curse does not end with a sword. It does not respect banishment or title. It waits in the blood, patient as stone, hungry as the grave.

And it is never, truly, satiated.
