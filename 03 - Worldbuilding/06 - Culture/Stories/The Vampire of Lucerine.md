# The Vampire of Lucerine
*A folk tale of the Northern Provinces, circa 1843*

In an age where old roads ran silver with moonlight, there stood the quiet town of Lucerine, nestled between foothills and frostbitten pines. It’s people were craftsmen and farmers, a simple folk who rose with the sun and drank in the eve. Of recent eves, however, Lucerine had begun to barre their doors when the stars would come out. For it had a secret that only the night could keep truly hidden.

They called it a Vampire.

It came first as a shadow stretched too long across the cobblstones. Then came screams. Folk would find neighbors in the morning, pale as winter leaves, every drop of life drained from their veins. Upon their necks were two small holes, no wider than a sewing needle, neat and terrible. No blood stained the ground. No wound bled. It was as if the body had been wrung dry from within, leaving nothing but a hollow shell and a room that smelled of copper and cold.

Those who glimpsed the beast and lived another day spoke of grey, leathery skin. Eyes that burned like coals plucked from a dying fire. Limbs elongated and twisted, moving with a skittering grace that made the stomach churn. It wore little clothes, torn asuner, with scratches all over it’s skin as though it shred them itself.

The creature that haunted Lucerine was both of no den or ditch. It was once man. The brother of Duchess Lenora Venn – a gentleman of refined tastes and gentle bearing, husband to a soft-spoken merchant’s daughter, and father of a little girl. His name had been Alaric, and by all accounts, he was kind.

The Venn family, however, carried a stain older than their title.

They called it the Sanguine Curse, though no one outside of their lineage knew it at all. It was a rot that slept in the marrow, generation after generation, waiting for the moment when the body’s magic bloomed. For in this world, blood holds more than water. It is the road upon which magic travels, the river that carries power to every finger and thought. And blood, being the vessel, is the finest catalyst known to the old arts.

When Alaric’s curse awakened, it did not merely change his shape. It rewrote his mind.

The power held in another’s blood became and intoxicant he could not refuse. By drinking deep, he did not simply take life – he stole the magic that flowed within, absorbing those he fed upon like a sponge taking water. The creature would walk away strong, faster, and more terrible than before, the power knitting itself into his cursed flesh.

Aleric fought this curse with all of his might, only thinking of his wife and daughter. But the toll of such a curse was more than his mortal mind could endure.

When the first moon passed and he woke with blood on his lips, no memory of the night before, Aleric begged his sister to lock him away. The Duchess, torn between duty and love, sealed him in the deepest celler of the keep, behind iron doors and warding glyphs. But a curse that burrows into it’s hosts’s mind is a clever thing. Aleric would barracade himself one evening, weeping apologies to his wife and daughter, and by midnight his hunger would peel him open from within. The thing that broke out of the cellar was no long Aleric. It was the Vampire.

For three winders, the town lived in terror. No one walked alone after dark. Windows were painted black to hide the candlelight. Mothers told their children that the Vampire did not exist, even as they checked locks twice and slept with knives beneath their pillows. The tavern emptied by sundown. The night watch quit in droves, for what good is a torch against such a beast.

The Duchess tried to govern as though nothing were wrong, but whispers followed in her wake. Of her brother gone missing. Of her blood. Her curse. She had his Aleric’s wife and daughter in the eastern wing of the keep, protected by guards at all hours of the day.

And then came the Knight.

His name lost to history – he arrived on a roan horse with armor that had seen southern desserts and eastern seas, bearing a sword that hummed with enchantment. He had heard of the Vampire three towns over, and he had come to do what local courage could not.

The knight did not wait for the creature to come to him. He was smart, and studied such patterns of attack. Mapping the streets where bodies were found, the trails of tracks that the creature would leave behind. All leading back to a single place: the keep.

On the fourth night, he did not patrol the town. The knight walked the path to the Venn estate, confronting them at the gate. He brought with him proof of his mistrust, and the Duchess laid the truth bare. She told him of the curse that afflicted their family. Of generations who managed to hide their affliction behind wealth and status. Of servents and prisoners who would vanish from record. She told him of Aleric, of the nights he chained himself with silver only to break free. Of the love he still held for his family even as the hunger scraped him hollow. She showed him the alchemical journals, the crude attempts at a cure written in languages no living scholar could read. And she showed him the cellar door, shaking on it's hinges, as something behind it seemed to smell the scent of the magic on the other side of the door.

The knight understood, then, that he had not been hunting a monster. He had been hunting disease made flesh.

But understanding and mercy were not always the same thing.

When the door shattered and the Vampire emerged – grey-skinned, crimson-eyed, ravenous with hunger – the knight met it with his sword. The battle was terrible but brief. The creature had stolen so much magic that it wielded powers no single beast should possess. But the knight was skilled, the blade was old, and in the end he struck true.

The Vampire fell.

As the red light left it’s eyes, the grey skin softened and the limbs shrank back to something almost human. For a moment, the knight saw a man’s face beneath the monster – a face lined with relief as much as pain. Then it was still.

Aleric Venn was dead.

The knight brought the news to the Lord Regent that oversaw the province, and the truth spread like plague itself. The Venn bloodline had been cursed for centuries, hiding their affliction behind titles and stone walls.

The Duchess was stripped of her titles. The family was banished from the keep they had held for nearly three hundered years, cast out with nothing but the clothes on their backs and the curse in their veins. The Lord Regent declared that no Venn would ever again hold land or office in his domain.

The knight recieved a medal he never wore. He left Lucerince by the same road he had entered, and some say he threw his enchanted sword in the river, having felt he lost his right to bear it after taking the life of one such as Aleric.

As for the town itself, the killings stopped. The night watch returned. Windows were unshuttered, and children played in the streets after sunset. Lucerince healed, as towns do.

But the curse was not truly over. Simply displaced.

The Venn family scattered to the winds, taking their secret with them. Blending in shadow and poverty. Somewhere, in some forgotten cellar, another brother or sister or nephew could be begging to be locked away. Another family who hides the truth behind locked doors. And somewhere, in another town, the smell of copper and cold magic fill the air after the full moon.

The Sanguine Curse does not end with a sword. It does not respect banishment or title. It waits in the blood, patient as stone, hungry as the grave.

And it is never, truly, satiated.
