# Sedrek
---

## Overview

The Sedrek is a large pterosaur-like creature, warm-blooded and highly intelligent, found historically across leyline-dense coastal cliffs and elevated wetland regions. It is not a bird, though it is frequently misclassified as one on first encounter. Its closest anatomical parallel is the pterosaur — a body plan independently arrived at through a separate evolutionary lineage, producing something that looks familiar and wrong in equal measure.

Sedreks are currently considered rare to the point of near-extinction in the wild. Most documented encounters are historical. Living specimens have not been reliably confirmed in several generations, though unverified sightings persist in remote regions.

---

## Anatomy

### Body

The Sedrek has a stout, barrel-shaped torso with a deep keeled sternum — a pronounced ridge running the length of the breastbone that anchors the powerful musculature required for its wing-arms. The body reads as compact and almost squat relative to its wingspan, which is misleading; the torso is dense with muscle and heavier than it looks.

### Neck & Head

The neck is the Sedrek's most distinctive feature. Roughly equal in length to the torso, it is composed of a high number of fine, interlocking vertebrae that grant it an extraordinary range of motion — functionally closer to a snake than a bird. A Sedrek can whip its neck with striking speed, coil it defensively around small objects or offspring, and rotate it through angles that read as deeply unnatural to a human observer.

The neck terminates in a conical, tapering skull that narrows toward a pointed rostrum. At the end of this rostrum sits the **hammer** — a broad, flat, chitinous structure that is not bone and not technically part of the living animal. It grows from the tip of the beak the way a toucan's bill does: a keratinous and chitinous extension that the creature sheds and regrows periodically. The actual mouth opens just before the hammer begins, meaning the hammer itself is never used for feeding directly.

The hammer serves two purposes. As a weapon, it delivers blunt, devastating strikes — the long neck acting as a whip to generate force. As a chamber, its hollow interior is used for short-term storage: small prey that hasn't been consumed yet, or young offspring being transported. Hatchlings can curl into the hammer and remain there safely for hours.

### Wing-Arms

The Sedrek has no true arms. Its forelimbs have evolved entirely into wing-arms — long, powerful appendages that function as both wings in flight and forelegs on the ground. A membrane runs from the torso to the full length of each arm, forming the wing surface. Chitinous structural elements — lighter than bone, semi-rigid — run through this membrane as stabilizers, fanning outward when the wing is unfurled and folding flat against the arm when furled.

The defining mechanical feature of the wing-arm is the **locking elbow joint**. When the Sedrek prepares to glide, the elbow twists and slots into a hooked crevice in the joint structure, locking the arm into a perfectly straight position. In this locked state the arm cannot bend — it becomes a rigid gliding surface capable of sustaining the creature across vast distances with minimal active movement. The joint unlocks with equal ease, and the transition between locked and articulated causes the creature no pain or apparent discomfort. Examining the skeleton, the locking mechanism is immediately visible: a small hooked recession in the joint bone where the opposing surface slots cleanly into place.

At the terminus of each wing-arm are three long, retractable claws. These are kept furled during flight and ground locomotion, extending only for climbing, grappling, or combat.

### Hind Legs

The hind legs are digitigrade — structured like a large cat or raptor, with a pronounced backward-bending joint and an elongated foot that contacts the ground only at the toes. They are noticeably smaller than the wing-arms and built primarily for perching on elevated surfaces. On the ground, however, they are fully functional for locomotion in the Sedrek's quadrupedal walking gait.

## Locomotion

### Flight

The Sedrek takes off by unfurling its wing-arms and generating lift with powerful downstrokes. Once airborne, it quickly locks its elbows into the gliding position and sustains altitude with minimal effort — riding thermals and air currents over long distances. Active flapping is reserved for takeoff, landing, and rapid directional changes. A fully extended Sedrek in locked-glide position has a wingspan approximately twice the length of its torso.

### Ground Movement

On the ground, the Sedrek folds its wing-arms tight against its body and drops into a quadrupedal stance, distributing weight across both the wing-arm wrists and the digitigrade hind legs. In this configuration it moves with a low, fluid gait that observers consistently describe as feline — the long neck carried forward and low, the body rolling with each stride. The transition from this walk to full flight takes only seconds.

## Behavior

Sedreks are solitary hunters that establish and defend large territories. They are ambush predators by preference, using the neck's speed and the hammer's force to stun or kill prey before it can react. Smaller prey is stored in the hammer chamber; larger kills are consumed immediately.

Despite their solitary nature, Sedreks demonstrate significant parental investment. Hatchlings spend their earliest weeks carried in the hammer chamber, emerging only when the parent lands to feed. The chamber's hollow interior maintains a stable temperature independent of external conditions, functioning as a natural incubator.

Sedreks are drawn to leyline-adjacent territory. Whether this is instinctive navigation toward stronger mana concentrations or a deeper biological dependency on leyline proximity is not fully understood. What is documented is that the historical range of the Sedrek maps almost precisely onto known leyline corridors — and that the contraction of their population over the past several centuries mirrors the periods of greatest leyline disruption.

## The Sanctum Specimen

A reconstructed Sedrek is displayed in Caldwell Hall at the Sanctum, mounted in a rotating series of poses. The official position of the school is that it has always behaved this way and requires no further explanation. The student body has accepted this answer to varying degrees.

Whether the specimen incorporates any original biological material or is a complete fabrication is a matter of ongoing informal debate among the student population. Faculty who know the truth have not volunteered it.

> Over time, the students have given a nickname to the creature in the hall, Shelly, said in an endearing and sometimes ominous fashion when talking about the rumors over whether the recreation is actually alive or not.
